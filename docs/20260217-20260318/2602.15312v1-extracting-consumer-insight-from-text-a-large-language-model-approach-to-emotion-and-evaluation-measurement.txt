Extracting Consumer Insight from Text: A Large Language Model Approach to Emotion and Evaluation Measurement Abstract 

Accurately measuring consumer emotions and evaluations from unstructured text remains a core challenge for marketing research and practice. This study introduces the Linguistic eXtractor (LX), a fine-tuned, large language model trained on consumer-authored text that also has been labeled with consumers’ self-reported ratings of 16 consumption-related emotions and four evaluation constructs: trust, commitment, recommendation, and sentiment. LX consistently outperforms leading models, including GPT-4 Turbo, RoBERTa, and DeepSeek, achieving 81% macro-F1 accuracy on open-ended survey responses and greater than 95% accuracy on third-party–annotated Amazon and Yelp reviews. An application of LX to online retail data, using seemingly unrelated regression, affirms that review-expressed emotions predict product ratings, which in turn predict purchase behavior. Most emotional effects are mediated by product ratings, though some emotions, such as discontent and peacefulness, influence purchase directly, indicating that emotional tone provides meaningful signals beyond star ratings. To support its use, a no-code, cost-free, LX web application is available, enabling scalable analyses of consumer-authored text. In establishing a new methodological foundation for consumer perception measurement, this research demonstrates new methods for leveraging large language models to advance marketing research and practice, thereby achieving validated detection of marketing constructs from consumer data. 

Keywords: large language models, consumer emotions, consumer evaluations, text analysis 2

1. Introduction 

Accurately measuring consumer perceptions at scale, especially those expressed through emotions and evaluations, remains a central challenge for marketing research. Perceptions linked to emotions such as anger, anxiety, and joy or to evaluations like trust and commitment deeply inform consumers’ decisions and firm outcomes (Cohen, Pham, and Andrade 2018; Gopinath, Thomas, and Krishnamurthi 2014; Gupta and Zeithaml 2006; Kranzbühler et al. 2020; Morgan and Hunt 1994; Palmatier et al. 2006). To identify such perceptions at scale, consumer-authored text, such as reviews and social media posts, provide extremely useful data (Babić Rosario et al. 2016). Yet most text mining methods applied to consumer reviews and posts seek to reduce this vast content to simplistic, coarse (positive versus negative) sentiment labels, offering limited insight into consumers’ different emotions and evaluations. Consequently, the richness of unstructured consumer data is severely underutilized. The need for methods that deliver valid, fine-grained, consumer perception metrics from unstructured data sources is urgent (Kim, Lee, and McCulloch 2024). Marketing research has largely tended to rely on text mining methods, that are not able to capture the full breadth and depth of how consumers express emotions and evaluations. For example, lexicon-based approaches such as LIWC (Pennebaker et al. 2015), the Evaluative Lexicon 2.0 (Rocklage, Rucker, and Nordgren 2018), metaphors (Luri, Schau, and Ghosh 2023), natural affect detection (Hotz-Behofsits, Wlomert, and Nabout 2025), and VADER (Hutto and Gilbert 2014) can detect affect or sentiment at a broad level but cannot capture more granular constructs such as discontent, excitement, love, trust, or commitment. Transformer 

models, such as BERT and RoBERTa (Chintagunta, Hanssens, and Hauser 2016; Lu and Kannan 2025; Netzer et al. 2012; Puranam, Kadiyali, and Narayan 2021), offer more linguistic precision, yet they have not been developed to detect specific marketing constructs, validated 3

using established marketing scales (e.g., three-item trust scale from Morgan and Hunt 1994), or benchmarked against consumers’ self-reports. Recent developments in large language models (LLMs), such as GPT, Llama 3, and DeepSeek, promise new possibilities for extracting insight from unstructured data, but their applications mainly have focused on generative tasks, such as visual product design (Sisodia, Burnap, and Kumar 2024), synthetic environments (Yang et al. 2023), or data generation (Arora, Chakraborty, and Nishimura 2024), not the validated detection of marketing relevant constructs and consumer perceptions. We know of no prior research that has fine-tuned LLMs to extract consumer emotions or evaluations, in an empirically valid way based on established marketing scales. This gap represents a methodological challenge; it also presents an opportunity to derive novel insights from consumer text data. To address both the gap and the opportunities, we develop the Linguistic eXtractor (LX), a fine-tuned version of the Llama 3.2-3B Instruct LLM that has been trained on consumer self-reports (see the Practical Implications section later in the paper). It is designed specifically to detect two types of marketing-relevant consumer perceptions: discrete emotions (e.g., joy, discontent, fear) and evaluations (e.g., trust, commitment, recommendation, sentiment). Both categories of perceptions are foundational to understanding consumer behavior and relationships (Morgan and Hunt 1994; Palmatier et al. 2006), in that emotions capture the affective dimension of consumption, while evaluations reflect more deliberative assessments. As a novel feature of our fine-tuning method, we train LX on consumer-authored text directly, paired with those authors’ responses to established marketing scales measuring their perceptions, to derive training data without requiring input from third-party coders. As a result, we show, LX more accurately identifies nuanced consumer perceptions that tend to be overlooked by lexicon-based tools or general-purpose LLMs. 4

To establish the viability of LX, we start by testing whether it can detect self-reported perceptions from open-ended survey data more accurately than existing models, such as GPT-4, DeepSeek, or RoBERTa. With Study 1, we also present a novel method for fine-tuning an open-source LLM (for this study, Meta AI’s Llama 3.2-3B Instruct), which we designed specifically to identify 16 consumption-related emotions (Richins 1997) and four evaluations (e.g., trust, commitment, sentiment, recommendation). Whereas traditional methods rely on third-party online reviews labeled by human coders (Khan et al. 2025), we leverage survey input to align consumer-authored text with their own scaled responses. Specifically, with a large-sample, open-ended survey of 2,523 consumers from the United States, Canada, United Kingdom, and Australia, we obtained reports of recent product or service experiences from consumers who also rated their emotions and evaluations using established marketing scales (Morgan and Hunt 1994; Palmatier et al. 2006; Richins 1997). A performance evaluation of LX’s prediction includes comparative assessments with transformer models (BERT, RoBERTa), state-of-the-art LLMs (GPT-3.5 Turbo, GPT-4 Turbo, and DeepSeek), and pretrained but not fine-tuned versions of Llama 3.2. The results confirm that LX outperforms the competing models in detecting 13 of the 16 emotions and all of the evaluations. Its macro-F1 score of 81% exceeds the 69% for GPT-3.5-Turbo and 68% for GPT-4 Turbo, for example. Considering LX’s fewer parameters and cost-free implementation, these results are promising. In Study 2, as a test of its generalizability, we check if LX outperforms existing models when applied to third-party–annotated customer review data from Amazon and Yelp. Trained human coders labeled the data sets, involving 3,710 Amazon and 2,193 Yelp online reviews, for the presence and polarity of marketing-relevant perceptions. These were used to further fine tune LX. Applied to separate holdout samples of 4,131 Amazon and 1,258 Yelp reviews, LX achieves macro-F1 scores in excess of 95%, substantially greater than all other models. 5

Study 3 offers nomological validity by presenting an application to customer clickstream and purchase data from a large U.K. online retailer, which we obtained from Wharton Customer Analytics. With this study, we seek to determine if the different consumer emotions relayed in customer reviews can predict downstream, business-relevant outcomes. This data set includes detailed records of product page views, review reading behavior, and transactions linked to more than 10,000 products. To model how LX-detected emotions in customer reviews might be linked to product ratings and, through those ratings, consumer purchase decisions, we use a system of seemingly unrelated regression equations with product-level controls. The results reveal that 12 of the 16 emotional effects are mediated by product ratings, but some emotions (e.g., discontent, peacefulness) are associated with purchases directly. Thus, adding to ongoing research on consumer emotions (Bagozzi, Gopinath, and Nyer 1999; Puccinelli et al. 2016), we demonstrate that consumer emotions can provide meaningful signals, beyond product ratings. Finally, to support its broad adoption, we deploy and demonstrate LX as a no-code, cost-free, web application that allows users to upload consumer-authored text in standard formats (e.g., *csv) and receive outputs that indicate the presence of 16 consumption-related emotions and key evaluative indicators (e.g., trust, commitment). This tool can improve the accuracy and scalability of consumer perception measures, extract new insights from unstructured consumer text data, and democratize access to advanced text mining methods. 

2. Consumer Perception Measurement Using LLMs 

Consumer emotions and evaluations are central to marketing research in various fields, such as relationship marketing (Morgan and Hunt 1994), customer loyalty and equity (Gupta and Zeithaml 2006; Palmatier et al. 2006), and affective decision-making (Cohen, Pham, and Andrade 2018; Kranzbühler et al. 2020). A consumer emotion is defined as “a 6

valanced affective reaction to perceptions of situations” (Richins 1997, p. 127; see also Ortony, Clore, and Foss 1987), which can influence both reactions and behaviors. Using the term consumer evaluations, we refer to consumers’ assessment and opinion of a firm or offering, such as trust, commitment, recommendation, or satisfaction (with its contemporary equivalent being sentiment), that similarly influence consumer reactions and behaviors 

(Gupta and Zeithaml 2006; Morgan and Hunt 1994; Palmatier et al. 2006) . A comprehensive review of six leading marketing journals 1 from 2000 to 2024, seeking any articles mentioning these terms in their titles, abstracts, or keywords, affirms the strong interest in such consumer perceptions, largely consistent over time (Figure 1). Notably, 14% (1,049 articles) of all published articles during this time period in these six journals mention at least one of these perceptions, with sentiment/satisfaction (43.6%) and emotions (17.2%) being the most frequently studied (for details, see Web Appendix A).

Yet across these substantial research streams, most studies continue to measure perceptions using survey-based scales, which may be well-validated but also are constrained in their scalability and context. The widespread availability of consumer-authored text, such as product reviews and social media posts, offers an alternative and rich source of data for revealing these perceptions, at scale, but it remains underutilized. Some marketing researchers have sought access by undertaking automated text analyses, but thus far, the available tools remain unable to deliver fine-grained measures purposefully developed to uncover specific marketing constructs. Dictionary-based tools (Hutto and Gilbert 2014; Pennebaker et al. 2015; Rocklage et al. 2018) rely on fixed lexicons to detect, for example, a few, psychologically relevant positive or negative affect categories; they cannot isolate discrete consumer emotions or evaluations like discontent, excitement, or trust. Transformer 

models, such as BERT and RoBERTa (Chintagunta, Hanssens, and Hauser 2016; Netzer et al. 2012; Puranam, Kadiyali, and Narayan 2021), offer greater linguistic flexibility, but they have not been adapted to detect marketing constructs, nor validated against self-reported consumer perceptions. To understand how these constructs have been text-mined in prior work, we conducted a second review of the same six journals, seeking studies that describe using text analyses, topic modeling, or natural language processing (Web Appendix A, Figure A2). The 115 articles we found were mostly published after 2018, and only 27 of them examined consumer emotions or evaluations: 14 focused on sentiment, 4 on a handful of emotions, 8 on both, and just 1 used text mining to detect recommendations (Table A2, Web Appendix A). This narrow focus on sentiment excludes considerations of the broader, more nuanced constructs that marketing theory tends to emphasize. Furthermore, these studies mostly leverage existing dictionary-based tools (n = 16) or neural networks with deep learning techniques (n = 4). None apply LLMs. This 8

methodological constraint has significant theoretical implications. Without scalable, theory-aligned tools that can extract emotions and evaluations from consumer-authored text, researchers cannot effectively test or extend core marketing models to large-scale, real-world settings. This constraint also might be why existing work rarely integrates textual insights with behavioral outcomes, such that we continue to wonder if textually communicated consumer perceptions can predict marketing-relevant outcomes, such as product ratings or purchase behavior. Furthermore, while consumer emotions and evaluations are frequently modeled as predictors of consumer choice, satisfaction, or brand engagement, existing studies often rely on data collected in small-scale, controlled environments. Many foundational studies draw on self-reports from 100–300 participants (e.g., Bagozzi, Gopinath, and Nyer 1999; Palmatier et al. 2006; Puccinelli et al. 2016), which are useful for theory testing but limited in behavioral realism. A text mining method that allows for the validated measurement of consumer emotions and evaluations can be operationalized across tens of thousands of actual text-based communication and conversation instances, helping to bridge the gap between internal validity and ecological validity for emotion research in marketing. Existing LLMs such as GPT, Llama 3, and DeepSeek offer great possibilities for extracting insights from unstructured text data sources, but they have not been fine-tuned or validated to measure marketing-relevant constructs such as consumer emotions and evaluations perceptions. 

3. Overview of Studies 

To address these critical gaps, we develop the Linguistic eXtractor (LX), a fine-tuned large language model trained to detect marketing-relevant consumer emotions and evaluations from unstructured text. To test its applicability, we conduct three empirical studies. Together, the studies demonstrate the theoretical applicability, practical utility, and 9

behavioral relevance of the click-based LX web app, as a new method for extracting fine-grained, theory-aligned consumer perception metrics from unstructured text data sources. Despite the growing use of large language models (LLMs) in marketing research, there remains no standardized framework for developing and validating fine-tuned models that measure core marketing constructs from unstructured text. To address this shortcoming, Table 1 offers a generalizable, step-by-step procedural guide to support future researchers in training and validating LLMs for this purpose. Although the roadmap reflects our own empirical implementation, it is designed as a flexible blueprint potentially applicable to the future development of LLM based measures of a wide range of relevant marketing constructs, including brand trust, perceived value, loyalty, service quality, authenticity, or sustainability perceptions. Each step outlines its purpose, required activities, and methodological standards to ensure construct validity, generalizability, and theoretical alignment. The three empirical studies that follow integrate this framework. Study 1 addresses construct specification, self-report-based labelling, and model fine-tuning. Study 2 evaluates generalizability by testing performance on third-party annotated review data. Study 3 examines nomological validity by assessing whether LLM-derived outputs relate to real-world outcomes such as product ratings and purchase behavior. Together, these studies also demonstrate how the steps in Table 1 can guide researchers in developing and validating LLMs for construct-valid, scalable measurement in marketing contexts.

4.1 Data Collection and Survey Design 

Traditional methods for training LLMs to detect consumer emotions and sentiment rely on third-party text annotated by human coders, which creates several challenges. First, consumers who review a product or service generally describe only one or two perceptions, so limited informational content is available. Second, the coders’ interpretations might not align with the author’s original intent. Third, substantial coder disagreements demand extensive resolution efforts. For instance, Khan et al. (2025) report that coding 11,800 software reviews for eight emotions took seven hours per coder, with a 42.1% disagreement rate that required further reconciliation. To overcome these limitations, we conducted a large-scale survey on Prolific, a platform known for high-quality data collection. We recruited 2,800 respondents from the United States, Canada, United Kingdom, and Australia, whom we compensated at a rate of £9 per hour for a 15-minute survey. Data quality checks led us to exclude 277 responses due to failed attention checks, time-outs, or incomplete answers, resulting in a final sample of 2,523 valid responses (M age = 38.6 years, SD age = 12.96; 42.0% men, 56.6% women, 1.4% preferred not to disclose gender; see Web Appendix B for further details of the respondent profile.). These respondents had to write about marketing-relevant themes in relation to a product or service they had experienced in the past month, then evaluate each theme according to an established scale. To ensure respondents had prior experience writing about product or service perceptions, we prescreened them to have active memberships on major review platforms such as Amazon, TripAdvisor, or Yelp. Respondents were asked to write about a product or service experience in one of nine categories: retail, clothing/footwear, furnishings, restaurant, health, transport, communication, recreation, or insurance (Table B3 indicates the number of respondents who selected each category). Because the proposed LLM needs to detect multiple marketing-relevant themes, we 12 

structured the survey carefully, to capture respondents’ perceived emotions, trust, commitment, recommendation likelihood, and sentiments toward marketing-relevant aspects (price, product/service, place, promotion, or 4Ps) of the product or service they chose to review. Respondents began by writing open-ended responses (of at least 15 words), elicited by the following question: “For the product/service you chose, please describe the emotions you felt when you were using the product/service.” A new webpage then displayed their response, alongside a list of 16 consumption-relevant emotions from Richins (1997) (Table B4 contains the complete list of emotions). Respondents were then asked: “Which of the following emotions did you feel when you were using the product/service (you can choose multiple emotions)?” After describing and revealing their emotional reactions to the product or service, respondents completed similar description and evaluation exercises to reveal their perceptions of trust, commitment, recommendation likelihood, and sentiment toward marketing-relevant aspects (4Ps). Table 2 contains the instructions for all the emotion and evaluation themes. 

Along with requesting open-ended text, we provided respondents with a measurement scale for each evaluation theme, each of which contained three items in random order; an exception is recommendation likelihood, for which we relied on Reichheld’s (2003) single-item, 11-point scale (0 = “not at all likely to recommend,” 10 = “extremely likely to recommend”). Most items used seven-point Likert scales (1 = “strongly disagree,” 7 = “strongly agree). The complete list of measurement items in Table 3 displays their high Cronbach’s alpha values, which confirm that the scales are reliable. As Figure 2 reveals, the combination of open-ended text input with structured scale responses ensures that respondents can refer to their own written text when they complete the scale items. Furthermore, the survey dynamically adjusts the measurement items depending on whether each respondent has chosen to review a product or service. For products, it includes all the 13 

emotions, recommendation likelihood, and aspect-based sentiment; for services, it also features trust and commitment.

4.2 Model Development 

In the model development process, we fine-tune LX using the survey data set and prepare it for the multitask classification across consumer emotions and evaluations. Most LLMs are pretrained on extensive factual corpora, such as Wikipedia, to predict subsequent tokens in a text sequence based on preceding tokens (Alizadeh et al. 2025; Arora, 15 

Chakraborty, and Nishimura 2025). Such models like Llama 3.2 and GPT 4-Turbo exhibit strong general-purpose capabilities across various tasks (Alizadeh et al. 2025; Khan et al. 2025) but fine-tuning effectively enhances their performance for specific applications. Fine-tuning involves three steps: selecting a pretrained baseline LLM, prompt engineering to develop effective instructions to guide the LLM training, and parameter-efficient fine-tuning to improve prediction accuracy and computational efficiency by refining the model parameters. 

4.2.1 Baseline LLM 

Since being introduced by Touvron et al. (2023), Meta AI’s open-source Llama series has evolved, so we utilize the latest version, Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, as a base model. Llama 3.2 offers several key benefits: it is cost-free, sufficiently open-source to allow users to retain their private training data when undertaking fine-tuning, and multilingual, such that it supports task deployment in more than 30 languages (Kili Technology 2025). Although an 8-billion-parameter version exists, we opt for the 3-billion-parameter model, due to its advantageous memory usage, training flexibility, and accurate inferences for practical development.

Therefore, the model outputs will correspond to theoretically meaningful perceptions. First, an initial task entailed detecting any of 16 emotions within the provided text. For example, if the assigned task was to detect the emotion of “joy,” the straightforward prompt would read, “detect joy present or not in the text.” After processing the assigned text (e.g., “So impressed! The quality is outstanding, and it feels like it was made just for me. I trust this brand and will definitely buy from them again”), the LLM selects from two possible outcomes: “joy present” or “joy not present.” In the survey, respondents could list up to 16 emotions they felt during their product or service experience, and if they had indicated feeling “joy,” the LLM would be trained to predict this emotion accurately. 

Second, unlike emotion detection, which is a binary task, detecting the polarity of marketing-relevant evaluations (e.g., trust, commitment, recommendation likelihood) requires survey data spanning several scale items, so that it is possible to determine whether a respondent’s evaluation is positive, negative, or neutral. In the example in Figure 2, after reverse-coding responses to the first item, the values for the three trust items get averaged to obtain the score for each respondent. Average scores less than 4 are negative, those equal to 4 17 

are neutral, and if they are greater than 4, it indicates a positive evaluation. 2 However, the recommendation construct requires a different approach, so we adopt Reichheld’s (2003) classification (and labels): Scores of 6 or less are negative (detractors), 7 and 8 are neutral (passives), and 9 or higher are positive (promoters). To map the LX outputs to these validated constructs, we apply standardized classification rules, derived from established marketing scales. Third, for the aspect-based sentiment analysis, we seek to categorize the survey responses as they relate to each aspect of the marketing 4Ps. For example, Table 3 shows that price-related sentiment, is measured on a 7-point Likert scale with three items from Gaski and Etzel’s (1986) price perceptions construct: (1) “The product/service I bought was overpriced,” (2) “The price for the product/service was fair,” and (3) “In general, I am satisfied with the price I paid.” After reverse coding the first item, the LLM averages the scores and categorizes them: –1 for averages less than 4, 0 for exactly 4, and 1 for greater than 4. Summing these categorizations across all 4Ps produces an overall sentiment score, such that it is negative for sums less than 0, neutral for exactly 0, and positive for greater than 0. With these task definitions and labels, we can fine-tune the LX using supervised learning techniques, optimized for multitask performance.

5. Study 2: Benchmarking LX Using Publicly Available Review Data 

To evaluate the generalizability of the LX model, we apply it to actual, consumer-authored reviews from Amazon and Yelp. Building on Study 1’s demonstration of LX’s ability to detect validated emotions and evaluations from consumers’ self-reported survey text, Study 2 tests for LX’s accuracy when applied to third-party review data, which is critical for assessing the external and construct validity of the model. We seek to confirm its ability to identify marketing-relevant consumer perceptions, such as discontent, trust, and recommendation likelihood, from observed (rather than surveyed), unstructured, unsolicited consumer input. 

5.1 Data Sources and Annotation Protocol 

This study leverages two publicly available review data sets that have been employed in prior marketing and information systems research: Amazon product reviews (Ni, Li, and 

McAuley 2019; https://nijianmo.github.io/amazon/ ) and Yelp restaurant reviews 

(www.yelp.com/dataset ). From the Amazon data set, we randomly sampled 8,000 sentences 

for emotions, 8,000 for sentiment, and 2,000 for trust, commitment, and recommendation likelihood. We then excluded non-English sentences and statements unrelated to the product or service. This left 7,450 sentences coded for the presence or absence of each emotion, 7,306 sentences coded for sentiment (positive or negative), and 1,824 sentences coded for positive or negative mentions of trust, commitment, and recommendation. Because we did not apply any filters related to the product category, review length, or ratings, these samples support 24 

broad, representative evaluations of LX’s performance across text types and tones. To establish the labeled training and test sets, we implemented a multistep human annotation protocol. With a custom-built interface in Prodigy3, annotators coded sentences for the emotion detection, polarity detection for evaluations, and aspect-based sentiment detection for the four Ps (price, product, place, and promotion) tasks. For each sentence, coders first determined if a specific perception was present; if so, they assigned it a polarity label (negative = –1, neutral/no polarity = 0, positive = 1). Each sentence was independently annotated by two coders, who had undergone extensive training and calibration. The coders completed multiple training rounds (100 sentences per coder per task), involving two reconciliation rounds, after 250 sentences and then after a further 250 sentences. They annotated the remainder of the data independently. This protocol ensured consistent, theoretically meaningful construct labels, aligned with marketing literature. 

5.2 Intercoder Agreement 

To gauge intercoder agreement (ICA), we determined the proportion of cases in which both annotators fully agreed on both the specific emotion or evaluation being present (yes = 1, no = 0) and, for the evaluations, concurred about their polarity. As Table 6 reveals, the ICA values were very high for most emotion and evaluation perceptions; they were lower but still acceptable in relation to more complex or composite constructs, such as contentment, discontent, and sentiment. To ensure the strong quality of the data in the final training and test review data sets, we only include instances for which both coders fully agreed. For example, 94% of the 7,450 sentences labeled for “joy” showed full agreement, and for 514 sentences they coded yes (1) for the presence of joy across all the Amazon and Yelp reviews. We used half of these agreed coding observations for further training and the other half for testing. Furthermore, we balance the training and test data for the presence or absence of each emotion. For instance, Table 7 shows there are 458 sentences used when testing for “joy” in 25 

the Amazon test data set. In this case 229 have joy present while the other 229 do not. We train LX, according to the same procedure we described for the survey data, except that in this case, the ground truth is established by human coding.

5.5 Discussion 

According to Study 2, LX generalizes effectively to third-party online review data and maintains high classification accuracy for both product and service domains. The results are robust, though we also note that performance for low-frequency constructs (e.g., envy, loneliness) remains modest, in part due to their limited representation in the training and test data. Therefore, in Study 3 we examine whether LX-detected consumer perceptions generalize across text sources and also if they can predict downstream marketing-relevant outcomes, including product ratings and purchase behavior, on the basis of real-world clickstream and transaction data. 

6. Study 3: Linking LX-Derived Emotions to Product Ratings and Purchase Behavior 

Studies 1 and 2 established LX’s accuracy and generalizability for detecting validated consumer perceptions; Study 3 evaluates whether emotions extracted by LX from consumer-authored reviews can predict downstream key marketing outcomes with behavioral relevance. With a mediation model, we postulate that LX-detected emotions are linked to product ratings, which in turn are associated with the likelihood of purchase among subsequent consumers who read those reviews. This behavioral validation of LX also extends prior research on the role of customer reviews in shaping consumer purchase decisions (e.g., Liu, Lee, and Srinivasan 2019; 29 

Vana and Lambrecht 2021; Varga and Albuquerque 2024). Linking text-extracted consumer perceptions to product evaluations and purchase behavior can contribute to broader theories of affective influence. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first attempt to demonstrate that fine-tuned LLM outputs, based on validated emotions, can explain real-world consumer behavior in transactional settings. 

6.1 Data 

To identify the relevance of various emotions in consumer decision-making, we leverage customer browsing and purchase data from Wharton Customer Analytics, which pertain to a large, U.K., online retailer. 10 We obtain individual-level clickstream and transaction information for two broad departments—home and garden (e.g., sheets, pillows) and technology (e.g., TVs, printers)—for February and March 2015. Thus, we can track consumer browsing journeys, which usually start with a category search that then gets refined to particular products. Each product page displays the price, average rating and volume of reviews, and a product description, but detailed review text is not automatically displayed. Customers must scroll down to the bottom of the product page to find a tab they can click to read the full text of reviews. Because of this design, we can verify whether each user clicked to read review text. Of the 30,879 products in this data set, not all of them were browsed during the two-month window, and nearly 20% of products have no reviews. Notably, discontent, anger, and sadness emerge as especially damaging to product ratings; any sign of unhappiness in reviews appears to be a red flag for other consumers. This finding aligns with evidence that negative review content can strongly damage product attitudes and purchase intentions (Chevalier and Mayzlin, 2006; Ludwig et al., 2013; Varga and Albuquerque 2024). Yet some emotions with negative valence (worry and loneliness) have significant positive associations with ratings, which initially seems counterintuitive. On reflection and linked to the focal product categories, this finding may suggest that reviewers mention their sense of worry or loneliness to describe how products (e.g., comforters, pillows) help resolve those concerns. A technology customer might note that they “were worried about buying this device, but it worked perfectly.” In such cases, the emotion words (“worry,” “lonely”) do not signal dissatisfaction but instead highlight the positive outcome delivered by the product, resulting in a stronger positive rating. This example underscores the need to acknowledge and anticipate that the nuanced effects of specific emotions are not limited to their valence; the context and appraisal also matter (Berger and Milkman, 2012). Joy, contentment, love, and excitement in a review exhibit significant, positive associations with star ratings, a pattern that is in line with marketing evidence that positive affect improves customer evaluations and behavioral intentions (Bagozzi, Gopinath, and Nyer 1999). Two emotions that are not conventionally identified as negative, romantic love and peacefulness, indicate significant negative relationships with product ratings. Arguably, overtly sentimental or tranquil language in a review might signal to readers that the reviewer is not sufficiently focused on product performance. Again, this evidence highlights that discrete emotions offer unique information, and positive outcomes can sometimes be communicated through ostensibly negative feelings, or vice versa. With the two-stage modeling approach, we next use the Purchase model (Equation 5) to discern if and how the LX-detected emotions can predict actual purchases by review 34 

readers. This assessment affirms that a higher average rating is associated with purchase likelihood ( bj = .00472 , p =0.0005), consistent with substantial evidence that better ratings encourage sales (Babić-Rosario et al. 2016; Chevalier and Mayzlin, 2006; Liu, Lee, and Srinivasan 2019 ). The control variables also behave as expected and are in line with prior research (e.g., Liu, Lee, and Srinivasan 2019 ; Vana and Lambrecht 2021). For example, price has a significant negative effect on purchase (pricier products are less likely to be bought after reading reviews), whereas products with more reviews (volume), more views (popularity), and longer review length are purchased more often. Having established the link from ratings to sales, we consider how each emotion influences purchases through this mediator and offer clear evidence of mediation. That is, consumer emotions expressed in reviews indirectly drive purchase behavior through their impact on the product’s star ratings. For most emotions, the data support either a negative (negative emotions) or a positive (positive emotions) indirect association with purchase likelihood. Whereas emotions like anger or sadness reduce average ratings, which then lowers the probability of a subsequent purchase by review readers, emotions like joy or contentment elevate ratings, which increase purchases by readers. For example, Table 9 shows that anger exerts a significantly negative, indirect effect on purchase through ratings, because on the anger → rating path, it has a significant negative effect ( Sl = −.48189 , p < .0001), and then the positive rating → purchase path reveals a positive association of the average product rating with purchase behavior ( bj = .00472 , p =0.0005). If we also calculate the indirect effect of anger on purchase behavior through product rating, using the estimated index of mediation (Sl × b j ), the results establish a significantly negative, indirect effect, equal to −.00227 (SE = .00068, p < .0008). As recommended by Parker, Nouri, and Hayes (2011) , we calculate a 95% confidence interval (CI) for the indirect effect based on 1000 bootstrap samples. The CI of [-.00385, -.00102] does not contain 0. Nor is anger directly related to purchase behavior 35 

(bj = .00387, n = .51) . Thus, the average product rating fully mediates the negative relationship between anger and purchase behavior. Similar patterns arise for other negative emotions such as discontent and sadness (and, to a lesser degree, fear, romantic love, and peacefulness). On the positive side, emotions like love, contentment, joy, and excitement, as well as loneliness, exert significantly positive, indirect effects on purchases through ratings: They boost ratings and thereby encourage more purchases. From a theoretical standpoint, this behavioral validation indicates that affective review content can shape real economic outcomes (Ludwig et al. 2013; Vana and Lambrecht 2021). Beyond linking review valence or sentiment to sales, our results pinpoint which emotions are associated with these effects and also establish the predictive power of fine-grained, LX-identified emotions for aggregate consumer behavior. However, two notable exceptions feature partial mediation, namely, discontent and peacefulness. These emotions affect ratings, but they also exhibit a significant direct effect on purchase behavior even after we control for these ratings. For discontent, the direct coefficient is negative ( bj ,c = −.01789, p = .0001), which implies that when prospective customers read reviews signaling discontent, they are less inclined to buy, beyond the effects of the star rating. There appears to be something uniquely discouraging about discontented language in reviews, which might not be captured by the numeric rating. This insight accords with arguments that review text provides information beyond star averages and can influence consumer decisions independently ( Ludwig et al. 2013 ). For example, even if a product earns a respectable 4-star average, when the review content expresses a lot of discontent, consumers might hesitate, suspicious of unresolved issues. The result for peacefulness is perhaps even more surprising; it has a small but significant negative direct effect on purchase. We speculate that the reason might be 36 

analogous to our discussion of romantic language, such that a review emphasizing peacefulness might appear irrelevant or “too emotional” to consumers who seek concrete product information. Emotional content that mismatches product or category norms can undermine credibility (Rocklage and Fazio 2020), such that if shoppers encounter a review that describes an appliance or some other utilitarian product made the reviewer feel peaceful, they might question the reviewer’s objectivity or ability to evaluate functional aspects. Such skepticism could directly suppress purchase likelihood, regardless of the star rating. Because these emotional cues sway the reader, we find partial mediation, such that the emotion affects sales both through ratings and via a direct path. 

7. General Discussion 

This research addresses a central methodological challenge in marketing: the lack of validated, scalable tools for measuring consumer perceptions expressed in unstructured text in ways that align with established marketing scales. Marketers increasingly seek to use consumer-authored content, such as reviews and social media posts, but existing analytical tools tend to reduce such content to broad sentiment scores, or general emotion categories, overlooking discrete marketing relevant emotions and evaluations ( Cohen, Pham, and Andrade 2018 ; Gopinath, Thomas, Krishnamurthi 2014; Gupta and Zeithaml 2006; Kranzbühler et al. 2020; Morgan and Hunt 1994; Palmatier et al. 2006). Dictionary-based methods lack emotional granularity (Hutto and Gilbert 2014; Pennebaker et al. 2015); generic transformer-based models mostly have not been adapted to detect validated marketing 

constructs ( Chintagunta, Hanssens, and Hauser 2016 ; Netzer et al. 2012; Puranam, Kadiyali, and Narayan 2021 ). Therefore, we propose the Linguistic eXtractor (LX), derived by fine-tuning Meta’s Llama 3.2-3B Instruct LLM on consumer-authored text, together with their self-reported perceptions measured using established marketing scales. Because this fine-tuned LX tool can detect 16 discrete consumption-related emotions (Richins 1997) and four 37 

core consumer evaluations (Morgan and Hunt 1994; Palmatier et al. 2006), it enables accurate, theory-consistent extraction of marketing-relevant constructs from unstructured text data sources, with broad research and practical implications. 

7.1 Research Implications 

Across three studies, we assess LX’s performance in relation to three central research questions: whether a fine-tuned LLM can reliably detect marketing-relevant perceptions defined by validated consumer self-reports, whether it outperforms existing tools in accuracy and generalizability, and whether LX-extracted perceptions can predict downstream marketing outcomes. S tudy 1 demonstrates that LX accurately identifies emotions and evaluations in open-ended survey responses and outperforms transformer and proprietary LLM baselines, including GPT-4 Turbo, DeepSeek, and RoBERTa, achieving 81% macro-F1 accuracy across 20 classification tasks. Study 2 confirms LX’s generalizability using third-party-annotated reviews from Amazon and Yelp, for which it offers greater than 95% F1 accuracy. Study 3 evaluates the nomological validity of LX outputs using clickstream and purchase data from a U.K. online retailer and details how emotions extracted from customer reviews predict product ratings, which in turn predict purchase behavior. Adding to consumer emotion research ( Bagozzi, Gopinath, and Nyer 1999; Puccinelli, et al. 2016), we find most emotion effects are mediated by ratings, though discontent and peacefulness also directly associated with purchase behavior, indicating that emotions in text are linked to decision-making beyond star scores alone. These findings advance marketing theory and methods in several ways. For example, they extend literature on consumer emotions and evaluations (Bagozzi, Gopinath, and Nyer 1999; Richins 1997) by introducing a scalable, validated method for detecting fine-grained perceptions from consumer-authored text. This study enables behavioral validation of text-derived consumer emotion constructs at a scale rarely observed in prior marketing research. 38 

Earlier work typically involved sample sizes in the hundreds and relied on self-report data (Palmatier et al. 2006), limiting their ability to generalize to marketplace behavior. Here, we analyze more than 10,000 products and thousands of product reviews and consumer browsing sessions, linking specific emotions in reviewer text to actual outcomes including ratings and purchases. This illustrates not only the predictive utility of LX, but also adds to research on the behavioral consequences of affect in consumer-generated content (Berger and Milkman 2012; Ludwig et al. 2013) by offering more fine-grained insights into how emotions influence real-world decision-making contexts. We also add to prior text analytics research (Netzer et al. 2012; Liu, Lee, and Srinivasan 2019) by demonstrating that when LLMs are fine-tuned on self-reported data, they outperform general purpose models. 

7.2 Practical Implications 

The LX model contributes to academic research and applied marketing by offering a validated, theory-aligned tool for extracting consumer perceptions from unstructured text. 14 

Unlike proprietary models such as GPT-4, LX is open-source, cost-free, and privacy-secure. Fine-tuned on self-reported marketing constructs, it produces outputs that align closely with theoretical frameworks central to marketing research. The no-code web application allows users to upload consumer-authored text files (e.g., *.csv), select relevant constructs, and receive batch-level output, detailing the presence and polarity of emotions and evaluations. It thus expands access to advanced text analytics by reducing the technical and financial barriers that arise from conventional, commercial LLMs. The LX web app translates raw consumer-authored text into numeric emotion and evaluation scores and dummies in four simple steps (Figure 3). 7.3 Limitations and Further Research 

In introducing and validating a new tool for extracting consumer perceptions from unstructured text, this research also faces some reasonable limitations. First, the meaning and impact of emotional expressions can vary across product categories and contexts; peacefulness or excitement are likely to evoke different meanings in wellness contexts than they do in the retail reviews we study for example. We hope continued research will delineate the effects of specific emotions, according to domains, platforms, or brand positioning. Second, additional applications of LX, such as to longitudinal data (e.g., customer reviews over time), post-crisis brand commentary, or repeat consumption contexts, could help reveal how emotions evolve and whether the shifts predict consumers’ behavior. Third, we train and validate the model with English-language data. Although LX’s architecture is theoretically grounded, and the Llama 3 LLM supports multilingual input, the generalizability of its outputs across languages has yet to be tested. Fourth, we explicitly developed LX to analyze consumer-authored text, but marketers increasingly rely on multimodal and firm-generated content too, in their efforts to monitor sentiment and engage customers. Building on recent developments for using LLMs for visual data (e.g., Sisodia, Burnap, and Kumar 2024), continued research might develop apps to mine consumer perceptions in other data modalities (e.g., audio, imagery). Ongoing uses of the new LX web app are likely to reveal novel opportunities for further investigations too. 42

As explained in the main text, a common criterion for LLM fine-tuning is the cross-entropy function:

where Pr  |  ,   , ,  is the conditional probability for the next token in the output sequence, given the input data, instructions, output thus far, and current model parameters . These probabilities are determined by the LLM on the basis of the full vocabulary (corpus), with probabilities calculated using the softmax function, which is similar to multinomial logit functions frequently used in marketing literature. For example, if a pretrained LLM vocabulary consisted of five words (joy, anger, discontent, not, present), these five words allow for the identification of three emotions, being present or not present. Table C1 is a schematic of the true output sequence for the online review in Table 1 in the main text, along with the vocabulary and conditional probability of each word in the vocabulary being the next token in the output sequence. 

Table C1. Pretrained Probabilities for Emotion Detection for Output Sequence “joy present” 

True labels joy present 

Vocab word index Position 1 probability Position 2 probability Vocabulary 

1 0.30 0.05 1 joy 

2 0.25 0.05 2 anger 

3 0.20 0.05 3 discontent 

4 0.20 0.40 4 not 

5 0.05 0.45 5 present 

For position 1 in the output sequence, the true token is joy , and it has the highest probability of any word in the vocabulary (.30). Using greedy sampling, the LLM selects the token with the highest probability ( joy in this case). In this pretrained LLM, there is not much difference in the probabilities for the three emotions in the vocabulary. For the second token in the output sequence, there also is not much difference between the probabilities for not and present , but the LLM choses present due to its slightly higher probability. Using Equation C1, the loss function for the pretrained LLM is ℒ = − log 0.3 − log 0.45 = 2.002 .After fine-tuning the LLM using data from the survey, in which consumers wrote about experiences that might elicit joy, this emotion indeed gets revealed as one of the emotions. In turn, the LLM’s parameters are updated, as are the probabilities for each token in the vocabulary. Table C2 gives the revised probabilities for the online review in Table 1. 11

This illustration outlines the basic principles by which LLMs update their conditional probabilities after being fine-tuned. It relies on prompts and data to update LLM parameters and thus minimize the loss function across all training data. 12

Web Appendix E. Details on Comparison Models 

In domains outside marketing literature, several transformer models and LLMs have been applied for emotion detection and sentiment analysis (e.g., Feng et al. 2024; Peng et al. 2023, Wake et al. 2023). We present several state-of-the-art models that have the capability to undertake the research tasks and that offer a high prediction standard: .

• BART 1: Lewis et al. (2019) developed the BART transformer model for text classification. When used as a zero-shot classifier (classification without further training), given an input text and a target perception, the output categories are constructed as candidate labels. The BART model then classifies the input text to the candidate label with the highest predicted probability. 

• RoBERTa 2: Another transformer model, it can be used in the same manner as BART for zero-shot classification. 

• Llama 3.2-8B-Instruct 3: This version of the Llama model contains 8 billion parameters and is trained to follow instructions, in the same way as the baseline model, Llama3.2-3B-Instruct. 

• GPT 3.5-turbo-instruct 4: This closed-source model, provided by OpenAI, has an estimated 20 billion parameters (OpenAI does not officially disclose the number). 5 We use this model in the same way as Llama3.2-3B-Instruct. It imposes costs of $1.50 per 1 million input tokens and $2.00 per 1 million output tokens. 6

• GPT 4-Turbo: This closed-source model provided by OpenAI has an estimated 1.76 trillion parameters. We use it in the same way as Llama3.2-3B-Instruct and GPT-3.5-turbo-instruct. The costs are $10.00 per 1 million input tokens and $30.00 per 1 million output tokens. 7

• Deep Seek -V3: A recently released open-source LLM with 671 billion parameters, it is pretrained on 14.8 trillion tokens. 8 The costs for using this model are $0.27 per 1 million input tokens and $1.10 per 1 million output tokens. The transformer models (BART and RoBERTa) are zero-shot, meaning they receive a prompt without any example being supplied. For the LLMs (Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, Llama3.2-8B-Instruct, GPT 3.5-turbo-instruct, GPT 4-Turbo, Deep Seek-V3), we use only pretrained versions. The results we present thus support comparisons of the pretrained Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct with a fine-tuned version (namely, LX), which in turn help demonstrate the benefits of our fine-tuning approach. Because GPT 3.5-turbo-instruct and GPT 4-Turbo are closed-sourced LLMs, they cannot be fine-tuned in the same way (for an explanation, see Ye, Yoganarasimhan, and Zheng 2025). Thus, we use only the pretrained versions of these OpenAI LLMs.
