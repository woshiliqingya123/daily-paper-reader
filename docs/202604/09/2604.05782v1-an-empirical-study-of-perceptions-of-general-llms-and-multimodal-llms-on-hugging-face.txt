## ABSTRACT 

Large language models (LLMs) have rapidly evolved from general-purpose systems to multimodal models capable of processing text, images, and audio. As both general-purpose LLMs (GLLMs) and mul-timodal LLMs (MLLMs) gain widespread adoption, understanding user perceptions in real-world settings becomes increasingly impor-tant. However, existing studies often rely on surveys or platform-specific data (e.g., Reddit or GitHub issues), which either con-strain user feedback through predefined questions or overempha-size failure-driven, debugging-oriented discussions, thus failing to capture diverse, experience-driven, and cross-model user perspec-tives in practice. To address this issue, we conduct an empirical study of user discussions on Hugging Face, a major model hub with diverse models and active communities. We collect and manually annotate 662 discussion threads from 38 representative models (21 GLLMs and 17 MLLMs), and develop a three-level taxonomy to systematically characterize user concerns. Our analysis reveals that LLM access barriers, generation quality, and deployment and in-vocation complexity are the most prominent concerns, alongside issues such as documentation limitations and resource constraints. Based on these findings, we derive actionable implications for im-proving LLM ecosystem. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

In recent years, general-purpose large language models (GLLMs) have been widely adopted across a broad range of applications, including natural language understanding [ 9 , 18 ], content genera-tion [ 21 , 45 ], and software development assistance [ 49 , 70 ]. Mean-while, multimodal large language models (MLLMs), which can pro-cess and integrate multiple modalities such as text, images, and audio, have rapidly emerged and attracted increasing attention from both academia and industry [ 10 , 67 ]. As both GLLMs and MLLMs gain widespread adoption, understanding how users per-ceive these models in practice becomes increasingly important. User perceptions reflect real-world experiences regarding model capabilities, usability, limitations, and expectations, and can provide valuable insights to guide the design and development of future LLM systems. Despite the growing body of research on user perceptions and experiences with LLMs, existing studies present several limitations. Prior work has explored user perceptions through online Reddit dis-cussions [ 39 ] and surveys [ 66 ], as well as domain-specific contexts such as education [ 8 ], healthcare [ 17 ], and LLM-based coding as-sistants [ 27 , 43 , 49 , 52 , 59 , 65 , 71 ]. However, these studies primarily focus on end users and some rely on predefined survey instru-ments, which may limit the diversity and authenticity of real-world feedback. Complementary research has investigated developer chal-lenges in LLM development and deployment. For instance, Yu et al. [ 68 ] analyzed GitHub issues of DeepSeek, LLaMA, and Qwen to identify failure symptoms and root causes in fine-tuning and deployment, while Chen et al. [ 16 ] examined challenges faced by developers building applications with GPT models based on OpenAI developer forum data. However, these studies typically focus on a limited set of models and predominantly capture failure-driven, problem-centric discussions, mainly reflecting debugging and trou-bleshooting processes. Consequently, a critical gap remains in un-derstanding the end-to-end LLM lifecycle—from model selection and configuration to fine-tuning and deployment—from diverse practitioners’ perspectives. To address these gaps, this paper presents an in-depth empirical study of user discussions on Hugging Face, covering both GLLMs and MLLMs. Unlike traditional code-hosting platforms or general forums, Hugging Face serves as the central hub for the modern AI ecosystem, bridging the gap between model developers and down-stream practitioners. It hosts a wide variety of models alongside active community discussions where users report not only technical bugs but also share deployment configurations, prompt engineering experiences, and practical adoption barriers. By systematically col-lecting and manually analyzing representative discussion threads across both GLLMs and MLLMs, we develop a three-level taxonomy to systematically categorize user concerns into seven dimensions: functionality, usability, reliability, supportability, performance, com-parison, and general experience. Overall, our analysis reveals several key insights into user per-ceptions of GLLMs and MLLMs, reflecting the current maturity, bottlenecks, and real-world dynamics of the LLM ecosystem. First, 

access and licensing barriers , particularly in the Llama model family, emerge as primary obstacles that disproportionately affect GLLM users. Second, generation quality remains the core dimension for evaluating model capabilities, with MLLM users showing higher sensitivity due to the added complexity of multimodal generation, where prompt and template usage further influence output quality. Activity Filtering and Manual Validation. Hugging Face pro-vides discussion forums where users report issues, share experi-ences, and request features, offering rich insights into real-world usage. After filtering out pull requests and system messages to retain only user-generated threads, we apply an activity threshold of 50 threads. This threshold strikes a necessary balance: a higher limit excessively narrows the scope of models, while a lower one lacks the active engagement required to yield valuable insights into user perceptions. Ultimately, this reduces our candidate pool from 1,280 to 60 active models. We then manually verify whether the remaining models correspond to GLLMs or MLLMs. Models outside the scope of our study, such as model plugins (e.g., h94/IP-Adapter-FaceID [ 32 ]), as well as models whose discussions are dominated by non-technical or politically oriented debates (e.g., perplexity-ai/r1-1776 [56]), are excluded. After this validation process, we obtain a final set of 38 models, including 21 GLLMs from 9 model families and 17 MLLMs from 13 model families (shown in Table 2). 

Sampling. Using the Hugging Face API, we collect all discussion threads associated with these models, treating each thread as one record. Each discussion thread consists of an initial post and may include multiple user replies; we treat the entire thread as a single analysis unit. In total, we obtain 4,250 records (2,808 for GLLMs and 1,442 for MLLMs). Non-English content is first translated into English using Google Translate, and extremely short posts (contain-ing fewer than 10 words) as well as image-only threads are then removed. After cleaning, 3,630 valid discussion threads remain, in-cluding 2,383 for GLLMs and 1,247 for MLLMs. Given the relatively large dataset, we apply statistical sampling to facilitate qualitative analysis, ensuring that the sampled threads are representative, man-ageable, and suitable for drawing reliable insights. Following prior empirical studies [ 30 , 48 , 49 ], we adopt a sampling strategy with a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error. From the GLLM discussion pool, we randomly sample 331 threads. To enable a bal-anced comparison between model types, we also randomly sample 331 threads from the MLLM discussions. ## 2.2 Manual Annotation and Taxonomy Construction 

Initial Discussion and Coding Principles. Initial Discussion and Coding Principles. To establish annotation principles, three expe-rienced authors (>3 years in LLMs) review a random 100-record pilot sample. We analyze both text and images to identify recurring themes, ensuring a comprehensive understanding of the dataset’s characteristics. This process leads to two key principles: (1) Multi-label annotation principle . A single discussion thread may contain multiple independent topics (e.g., hardware requirements followed by comments on generation quality). Therefore, when multiple distinct themes appear, multiple labels can be assigned to ensure complete topic coverage. (2) Three-level taxonomy principle . Dur-ing the pilot review, we observe that a simple two-level structure (category–leaf node) is too coarse to capture the diversity of user concerns. Following prior work [ 34 , 49 ], we organize the taxonomy into three levels— category , subcategory , and leaf node —to represent discussion topics with finer granularity. Based on insights from the pilot review and existing taxonomies in app review mining [ 40 ]and recent studies on LLM-based coding assistant reviews [ 49 ], we also establish an initial set of top-level categories, including 

Functionality , Usability , Supportability , Performance , and General Experience .

Bottom-Up Taxonomy Construction. Building upon the prede-fined top-level categories and annotation principles, we construct the taxonomy through a bottom-up process. The coding is con-ducted in sequential batches of 50 discussion threads. In each batch, two authors independently assign one or more labels to each thread based on its discussed topics. They then compare their labeling re-sults and resolve any disagreements through discussion with a third author. Semantically similar labels or distinct expressions referring to the same concept are merged and normalized into unified leaf nodes. Initially, these leaf nodes are directly grouped under the pre-defined top-level categories, forming a two-level structure. When a category accumulates more than four leaf nodes with clear thematic differences, an intermediate subcategory layer is inserted between the category and leaf nodes, resulting in a three-level taxonomy (cat-egory–subcategory–leaf node). The taxonomy is gradually refined as we proceed through the labeling batches. 

Taxonomy Refinement and Final Annotation. After complet-ing the first four batches (200 threads in total), we identify gaps in the taxonomy: several threads cannot be clearly categorized under the existing top-level categories. To address this issue, we introduce two additional top-level categories— Reliability and Com-parison —and revisit the previously annotated threads to reassign them to the appropriate categories. Following this refinement, the remaining 462 threads ( = 662 − 50 × 4) are independently labeled by the two authors. Any remaining disagreements are resolved through discussion with the third author. The inter-rater agree-ment for this phase reaches Cohen’s 𝜅 > 0.87 , indicating a high level of consistency between the two authors. In total, we manually annotate 662 discussion threads, resulting in 58 unique leaf nodes organized into 17 subcategories and 7 top-level categories. 

Sentiment Annotation. A single discussion thread may express multiple sentiments across different discussion topics. Therefore, instead of assigning one sentiment to each discussion thread, we conduct sentiment analysis at the leaf-node level. Each labeled leaf-node is marked as like , dislike , or neutral (including feature requests or emotionally neutral mentions). Considering that many technical discussions contain neutral content such as bug reports or usage questions, to mitigate additional bias introduced by subjective interpretation, a label is marked as like or dislike only when the user explicitly expresses a clear positive or negative sentiment. Two authors first independently annotate 20% of the data to establish a common understanding of the sentiment categories. Disagreements are resolved through discussion with the third author to reach a consensus. The remaining 80% of the data is then annotated independently by the two authors, with remaining disagreements again resolved through discussion with the third author. The inter-rater agreement for this stage reaches Cohen’s 𝜅 > 0.83 , indicating high consistency between annotators.

## 3.2 RQ2: What key dimensions of LLMs do users care about most? 

Following prior studies [ 19 , 55 , 60 ], we construct an 18 × 2 con-tingency table (rows: 17 subcategories plus General Experience ;columns: GLLM vs. MLLM) and perform a Pearson chi-square test. The results indicate a statistically significant difference in the dis-tribution of discussion topics between the two groups ( 𝜒 2 = 96 .45 ,df = 17 , 𝑝 < 0.001 ), where 𝜒 2 denotes the chi-square test statistic, df represents the degrees of freedom, and 𝑝 indicates the statisti-cal significance of the observed difference. To further identify the sources of these differences, we compute standardized residuals. An absolute residual value greater than 1.96 indicates that the ob-served frequency is significantly higher or lower than expected. The results reveal several notable differences. Functionality–Core Capabilities is significantly overrepresented in the MLLM group (residual = +4.15), while Usability–Access & Licensing is significantly overrepresented in the GLLM group (residual = +8.09). ## 4 IMPLICATIONS 

Implication 1: Optimizing Access Mechanisms for the Llama Model Family to Lower Barriers for Developers. As indicated in Finding 1, the primary barrier developers face when attempt-ing to use advanced models in the Llama family is not a lack of technical capability, but rather the cumbersome and opaque official application and approval process. User feedback highlights the in-efficiency of this mechanism. For instance, one user reports: “Hello, I’m a student at UCSD and need to use this model to complete a home-work assignment. My initial request for access was denied. Would it be possible to reconsider and grant me access?” (D305, GLLM). To address this issue, Meta could adopt tiered access control based on user identity and intended use. For students and independent researchers, the approval process could be simplified or restricted tiers introduced to lower entry barriers while maintaining safe-guards. In addition, inconsistent access policies across platforms further exacerbate this issue, creating unnecessary friction. For example: “Hi, I’m an AI researcher based in United States, and my access has been approved through meta website. However, my appli-cation for the same models was rejected on the huggingface platform.” 

(D49, GLLM). Establishing a unified identity authentication system across distribution platforms would help mitigate delays caused by redundant review processes [6, 58]. 

Implication 2: Establishing a Standardized Deployment Frame-work to Address Environmental and Compatibility Chal-lenges in Engineering Implementation. As indicated in Finding 3, the lack of standardization in model deployment is a critical bottleneck for scalable adoption and practical use [ 31 ]. User feed-back highlights issues such as inconsistent environments, depen-dency conflicts, cross-platform incompatibility, and missing param-eter guidance. The lack of standardized troubleshooting further increases overhead and failure rates. For instance, when deploying PaddleOCR-VL using Docker, a user encounters a blocking error 

“Unknown model: PaddleOCR-VL” due to a mismatch between the official container command and the model name, preventing suc-cessful deployment (D656, MLLM). To address these issues, model providers should offer a standardized deployment framework com-prising: (1) a unified dependency specification (e.g., compatible ver-sions of libraries such as transformers and torch), (2) cross-platform deployment scripts covering Docker, cloud environments, and local setups, (3) standardized parameter configuration templates, and (4) comprehensive troubleshooting guidelines for common errors. This standardization reduces deployment complexity, minimizes debugging effort, and improves efficiency and reliability. 

Implication 3: Strengthening Official Documentation and Ex-ample Code to Reduce Barriers to Model Onboarding and Basic Usage. As identified in Finding 4, deficiencies in official documentation, model cards, and accompanying example code sig-nificantly hinder users’ ability to learn and apply LLMs. Many models currently present common problems such as incomplete documentation systems, outdated content, and non-functional code examples [ 63 ]. Some users report: “I am facing the problem trying to run it with the code snippet from the model card.” (D429, MLLM). Moreover, there are complaints about the inaccessibility of docu-mentation: “Anyone also has the problem with ‘Read Model documen-tation’ link? For me it’s not working.” (D109, GLLM).Beyond basic usage, documentation gaps become more pronounced for advanced needs. Tasks such as fine-tuning on custom datasets, domain adap-tation, and framework integration often lack official guidance. As a result, even users proficient in basic operations struggle to extend models to their use cases. For example, one developer explicitly states: “I want to fine-tune it on my own data set, but there is no official fine-tuning documentation for the hf version.” (D357, MLLM). Model providers should prioritize high-quality, up-to-date docu-mentation and validated example code [ 7 ], with targeted guidance for advanced tasks (e.g., fine-tuning), to reduce adoption barriers and enable broader model use. 

Implication 4: Providing Official Quantized Versions in a Timely Manner to Lower Hardware Barriers and Acceler-ate Community Adoption. As indicated in Finding 6, one of the most prominent physical constraints developers face when deploying large-scale models is the substantial GPU memory re-quirement. When model providers release only full-precision ver-sions (e.g., BF16 or FP32), users with mid- to low-tier hardware are often unable to run the models due to out-of-memory issues. Although community-driven quantized versions are sometimes available, their performance and efficiency may not consistently meet practical expectations. For instance, a user reports that a community-provided quantized model exhibits no observable speed improvement: “just tested your 5.3 GGUF, and it’s exactly the same speed.” (D530, MLLM). Furthermore, developers explicitly express a preference for officially supported quantized releases, noting that: 

“I know the community will provide, but would be great to have ’official’ quantized versions.” (D149, MLLM). Model providers are encouraged to release official quantized versions early in the release cycle. Widely used formats such as GGUF(GPT-Generated Unified Format) [ 26 ], GPTQ(Generative Pre-trained Transformer Quantiza-tion) [ 25 ], and AWQ(Activation-aware Weight Quantization) [ 46 ]typically support 4-bit and 8-bit quantization. This practice im-proves trust and accessibility, reduces engineering overhead, and accelerates real-world adoption. 

Implication 5: Model Iteration Should Prioritize Alignment with Human Perception and Commonsense Preferences over Benchmark Optimization. Although many recent models achieve state-of-the-art performance on a wide range of public bench-marks [ 14 , 37 , 44 , 47 , 62 , 69 ], users in real-world settings tend to An Empirical Study of Perceptions of General LLMs and Multimodal LLMs on Hugging Face Conference’17, July 2017, Washington, DC, USA 

care more about whether model outputs align with human intu-ition, commonsense reasoning, and perceptual realism [ 51 , 64 ]. For GLLMs, users are quick to notice signs of overfitting in models optimized primarily for benchmark performance. For instance, one user sharply notes in a model comparison: “Qwen3 235b is a grossly overfit mess.” (D124, LLM). The user further argues that vendors overtrain models to boost performance in specific domains, which leads to weaker performance on broader general knowledge: “Nor-mally when you train on a larger corpus, in this case over 30 trillion tokens, broad knowledge and SimpleQA scores go up per parameter count. But Alibaba so grossly overfit coding, math, and STEM that its much older and smaller Qwen2 72b has vastly more broad knowledge and a notably higher SimpleQA score (~18).” (D124, GLLM). In con-trast, models that provide more natural and fluent interactions are preferred by the community, as reflected in comments such as: “It almost feels like you’re interacting with a human when using models like Gemini 2.5 & GPT4.5.” (D124, GLLM). In image generation and visual editing, perceptual naturalness and subject consistency are prioritized over auxiliary capabilities. Users often express frustration with outputs that do not match hu-man visual expectations: “In images where the face is still not properly visible, the habit of generating fake faces remains.” (D648, MLLM). Many users explicitly emphasize that character and scene consis-tency is far more critical than additional features like text-to-image generation or anime stylization: “The text-to-image, anime or other such capabilities are secondary to having consistency.” (D484, MLLM). Model development should go beyond benchmark gains, empha-sizing RLHF [ 53 ] and real-world evaluations. Improving alignment with human expectations—e.g., commonsense in text and realism in visual tasks—is key to sustained real-world impact. 

Implication 6: Strengthening Multilingual Foundational Ca-pabilities to Support the Global Deployment of LLMs. In global LLM deployment, users increasingly rely on models’ non-English language processing capabilities [ 33 , 41 ], making multilingual ro-bustness a key determinant of successful global adoption. User feedback on Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 confirms this trend: one user inquires, “Are you planning to release a version of Mistral-7B that is mostly pretrained in French?” (D22, GLLM), while a Japanese de-veloper notes, “I want to use Mistral for Japanese.” (D259, GLLM). Demand is strongest for Asian languages, whose linguistic dis-tinctiveness drives higher expectations for localized support. As one user urges: “Please support Asian languages. Japanese, Chinese, Korean, etc...” (D567, MLLM). These findings highlight the need for robust multilingual support. Enhancing semantic and cultural alignment is vital for global adoption and retaining diverse user communities. 

## 5 THREATS TO VALIDITY 

Internal Validity. The manual annotation and taxonomy construc-tion introduce potential bias. Although three experienced researchers conduct the labeling with multiple rounds of discussion and arbitra-tion, achieving high inter-rater agreement (Cohen’s 𝜅 > 0.87 ), their prior knowledge may still lead to confirmation bias, particularly when interpreting discussions on emerging MLLMs. In addition, the three-level taxonomy (category–subcategory–leaf node) is it-eratively developed, and the initial pilot annotation of 200 threads introduces a potential anchoring effect, which may constrain the emergence of new categories despite iterative refinement. 

External Validity. Our dataset is limited to Hugging Face and in-cludes only 38 highly active models (21 GLLMs and 17 MLLMs, each with at least 50 discussion threads), which limits generalizability to other platforms (e.g., ModelScope, PyTorch Hub) or proprietary models (e.g., GPT-4, Claude). Although 662 threads are randomly sampled with a 95% confidence level and 5% margin of error, the sample may not fully represent the broader LLM ecosystem. Nev-ertheless, the scale of the dataset is comparable to that of prior empirical studies on LLMs [ 49 , 66 , 68 ]. Furthermore, the removal of extremely short or image-only posts excludes some modality-specific feedback. 

Construct Validity. The operationalization of user concerns relies on the proposed taxonomy, which, despite iterative refinement into 7 top-level categories and 17 subcategories, still involves ambiguous boundaries. For example, distinctions between Generation Quality 

and Reasoning Ability may depend on subjective interpretation. In addition, sentiment labeling (like/dislike/neutral) is conservative and is only applied when explicit opinions are expressed, which may underestimate nuanced or implicit user dissatisfaction. 

Conclusion Validity. Our findings are supported by qualitative evidence from selected discussion excerpts. However, this process introduces potential selection bias, as examples that align with observed patterns are more likely to be reported, potentially over-looking contradictory cases and leading to partial interpretations of user needs.

## 7 CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK 

This paper presents an empirical analysis of user discussions on Hugging Face to better understand how GLLMs and MLLMs are perceived and used in practice. Our findings highlight several key challenges observed in real-world usage. Access and licensing bar-riers, particularly in the LLaMA model family, remain a primary obstacle for users. Generation quality continues to be the central criterion for model evaluation, with MLLMs exhibiting higher sensi-tivity due to the added complexity of multimodal generation. Model invocation and deployment represent the most common practical challenges, especially for MLLMs, reflecting their higher system complexity and lower ecosystem maturity. In addition, issues such as insufficient and error-prone documentation, the difficulty of fine-tuning, and resource constraints are frequently reported. Based on these findings, we derive a set of implications for improving the LLM ecosystem. For future work, we will extend our analysis to additional platforms to improve coverage and generalizability.
