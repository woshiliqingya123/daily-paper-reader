Abstract 

Understanding emotions in natural language is inherently a multi-dimensional reasoning prob-lem, where multiple affective signals interact through context, interpersonal relations, and situational cues. However, most existing emo-tion understanding benchmarks rely on short texts and predefined emotion labels, reducing this process to independent label prediction and ignoring the structured dependencies among emotions. To address this limitation, we in-troduce Emo tional Scen arios ( EmoScene ), a theory-grounded benchmark of 4,731 context-rich scenarios annotated with an 8-dimensional emotion vector derived from Plutchik’s basic emotions . We evaluate six instruction-tuned large language models in a zero-shot setting and observe modest performance, with the best model achieving a Macro F1 of 0.501 , high-lighting the difficulty of context-aware multi-label emotion prediction. Motivated by the observation that emotions rarely occur inde-pendently, we further propose an entanglement-aware Bayesian inference framework that incor-porates emotion co-occurrence statistics to per-form joint posterior inference over the emotion vector. This lightweight post-processing im-proves structural consistency of predictions and yields notable gains for weaker models (e.g., 

+0.051 Macro F1 for Qwen2.5-7B). EmoScene therefore provides a challenging benchmark for studying multi-dimensional emotion under-standing and the limitations of current language models. 

1 Introduction 

Emotional intelligence (EI) refers to the ability to recognize, understand, and regulate emotions in oneself and others (Mayer et al., 2004). Emotions play a fundamental role in human cognition, influ-encing decision making, social interaction, and be-havioral responses to environmental stimuli (Hieida et al., 2018). Because emotional signals are of-ten implicit and context-dependent, understanding them requires interpreting subtle linguistic cues, sit-uational context, and interpersonal relationships. In natural language processing, this ability is typically evaluated through emotion classification tasks that aim to measure a system’s capacity for Emotion Understanding (EU), namely the interpretation of emotions expressed within textual context (Sabour et al., 2024; Buechel and Hahn, 2017). Despite significant progress in affective computing, mod-elling emotions remains challenging because emo-tional expressions are subjective, context-sensitive, and often intertwined with social dynamics (Izard, 2002; Perloff, 1997). Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) have led to remarkable improvements across a wide range of NLP tasks, including text generation, translation, and question answering (Hagos et al., 2024; Ren, 2024). As these sys-tems become increasingly capable of producing fluent and contextually appropriate responses, a natural question arises: Do LLMs genuinely un-derstand emotions, or do they merely reproduce linguistic patterns associated with emotional lan-guage? Existing emotion understanding bench-marks provide only a partial answer. Datasets such as SemEval-2018 Task 1: Affect in Tweets (Mo-hammad et al., 2018), GoEmotions (Demszky et al., 2020), ISEAR (Troiano et al., 2019), and Emo-tionLines (Chen et al., 2018) have enabled large-scale evaluation of emotion classification systems. However, many of these datasets suffer from key limitations. First, inputs are often short sentences or social media posts that lack sufficient context to disambiguate subtle emotional cues. Second, they rely on predefined emotion labels that may not align with established psychological theories of emotion. Third, most evaluation protocols treat emotions as independent categories, ignoring the fact that emotional states frequently co-occur and influence one another. In this work we introduce Emo tional Scen arios

2 Related Work 

Emotion theories and psychological grounding. 

Emotions have long been studied in psychology as adaptive mechanisms that influence cognition, behavior, and social interaction (Damasio, 1998; Gross and Barrett, 2011). Emotional intelligence refers to the capacity to recognize, interpret, and manage emotional information in oneself and oth-ers (Mayer et al., 2004). A large body of work has explored how emotional experiences are structured and expressed in human cognition. For example, emotional granularity describes the ability to distin-guish subtle affective states and has been linked to improved emotional regulation and social function-ing (Hoemann et al., 2021). Psychological theories such as Plutchik’s psychoevolutionary theory fur-ther suggest that complex emotional states arise from combinations of a small set of fundamental emotions (Plutchik, 1982, 1980). These theoretical perspectives highlight that emotions are inherently structured and often co-occur rather than appearing in isolation. 

Emotion understanding in NLP. Emotion anal-ysis has been widely studied within natural lan-guage processing as an extension of sentiment anal-ysis. Early work focused on detecting affective states in text through lexicons and classification models (Mohammad and Turney, 2013). Subse-quent research expanded this task to emotion recog-nition in conversations and context-dependent set-tings, emphasizing the importance of discourse structure and speaker interactions (Fu et al., 2023). Recent work has also explored multimodal ap-proaches that combine textual, acoustic, and vi-sual signals to improve emotion recognition and generation in dialogue systems (Liu et al., 2024; Chandraumakantham et al., 2024). These studies demonstrate that emotional signals are often dis-tributed across multiple modalities and contextual cues. 

Emotion datasets and benchmarks. The devel-opment of emotion understanding systems has been driven by the availability of annotated datasets. Early benchmarks such as the SemEval affec-tive text tasks focused on identifying emotions in short texts such as tweets (Strapparava and Mi-halcea, 2007; Mohammad et al., 2018). More re-cent datasets, including GoEmotions (Demszky et al., 2020), EmotionLines (Chen et al., 2018), and crowdsourced corpora derived from ISEAR (Troiano et al., 2019; Scherer and Wallbott, 1990), have introduced larger taxonomies of emotions and conversational contexts. Despite these advances, most datasets rely on short or decontextualized in-puts and predefined emotion categories, which may limit their ability to capture complex emotional states. In addition, annotation practices vary widely across datasets, ranging from crowdsourcing to ex-pert labeling, which can affect label consistency and reliability. 

Emotion understanding in large language mod-els. With the rise of large language models (LLMs), recent research has begun to examine their capacity for emotional intelligence. Studies have evaluated LLMs on emotion recognition, empa-thy, and affect-aware reasoning tasks (Sabour et al., 2024; Sorin et al., 2024). Other work has investi-gated how LLM architectures represent emotional information internally or handle ambiguous emo-tional cues (Hong et al., 2025; Reichman et al., 2026). These findings suggest that while LLMs demonstrate promising capabilities in affective lan-guage tasks, their performance remains sensitive to context and evaluation design. 

Limitations of existing benchmarks and our ap-proach. Despite substantial progress, existing emotion benchmarks exhibit several limitations. Many datasets rely on short or decontextualized texts that lack sufficient contextual information to disambiguate emotional states. In addition, most evaluation protocols treat emotion prediction as a collection of independent classification decisions, ignoring the structured dependencies among emo-tions suggested by psychological theories. As a re-sult, it remains unclear whether models genuinely reason about emotional states or merely detect surface-level affective cues. To address these lim-itations, we introduce EmoScene , a benchmark of context-rich emotional scenarios grounded in Plutchik’s theory of emotions. Each scenario is an-notated with an 8-dimensional emotion vector rep-resenting combinations of fundamental emotions. We further model dependencies among emotions using a Bayesian inference framework that incorpo-rates statistical co-occurrence patterns observed in the dataset, enabling emotion understanding to be studied as a structured multi-label inference prob-lem. 

3 EmoScene Dataset 

Understanding emotions in natural language re-quires reasoning over contextual cues, interper-sonal interactions, and physical states described within a scenario. However, many existing emo-tion benchmarks rely on short, decontextualized Figure 2: Overview of our steps to construct EmoScene dataset 

texts, which makes it difficult to disambiguate sub-tle emotional signals and often reduces emotion prediction to a fixed set of predefined labels. To address this limitation, we introduce Emo tional 

Scen arios ( EmoScene ), a benchmark consisting of 

4,731 context-rich scenarios designed to evaluate multi-dimensional emotion understanding. Each example contains a subject description and a sce-nario describing the surrounding situation, and is annotated with an 8-dimensional binary emotion vector grounded in Plutchik’s psychoevolutionary theory of emotions (Plutchik, 1982, 1980). This representation corresponds to Plutchik’s eight basic emotions— joy, trust, fear, surprise, sadness, dis-gust, anger, and anticipation —and allows multiple emotions to co-occur simultaneously, enabling the dataset to capture complex emotional states while supporting multi-label emotion prediction. The dataset was constructed in two stages: sce-nario generation and human annotation . An overview of the pipeline is shown in Figure 2. We leverage the Emotional Commonsense Knowl-edge Graph (ECoK) (Wang et al., 2024) as the starting point for scenario creation. ECoK is a knowledge graph designed to represent emo-tional knowledge using <head, relation, tail> 

triples. The head entities describe emotional events, while the relations capture different aspects of emo-tional experience. The graph defines seven relation types: group, causes, bodily symptoms, feelings, expression, action tendencies, and advice .For each head node in ECoK, we generate one scenario using GPT-4o. The model is prompted to construct a short narrative describing a subject experiencing an emotionally relevant situation, in-corporating contextual cues such as environmental conditions, interpersonal interactions, and bodily responses. After generation, the scenarios were manually reviewed to remove duplicates and in-consistent outputs. This filtering process resulted in a final dataset of 4,731 scenarios . The gener-ation prompt used for this process is provided in Appendix E. Expert annotators have gone through every scenario and labeled the instance. Overall Fleiss kappa ( κ) is 0.43 which falls in the moder-ate agreement range. More details on the Human Annotation is in the Appendix: B. 4 Methodology 

Traditional multi-label classification frameworks typically model each emotion as an independent Bernoulli variable, implicitly assuming that emo-tional states occur in isolation. However, this as-sumption is fundamentally misaligned with the na-ture of human affect, where emotions are rarely independent and often co-occur in structured, inter-dependent ways. For instance, emotions such as joy and surprise may reinforce each other, while oth-ers, like anger and sadness, may exhibit complex conditional relationships depending on context. To address this limitation, we introduce the con-cept of Emotion Entanglement, which captures the inherent dependencies and interactions among emo-tions as a coupled system rather than a set of dis-joint labels. Building on this perspective, we pro-pose an Entanglement-Aware Bayesian Inference framework that explicitly models the joint distribu-tion over emotions. By incorporating these depen-dencies into the inference process, our approach enables more coherent, context-sensitive predic-tions that better reflect the underlying structure of human emotional expression.

6 Results & Analysis 

In this section, we discuss the evaluation of mul-tiple instruction-tuned open-source LLMs on the EmoScene benchmark for multi-label emotion pre-diction using Scenario and Subject. We compare zero-shot performance with our proposed Bayesian Inference (BI) approach and report the results in Table 1. Performance is assessed using Lexical Accuracy, Vector Accuracy, Hamming Loss, and Macro F1-score, capturing label-wise correctness, exact set match, label errors, and balanced per-formance across emotions. The prompt used for evaluation is included in the Appendix 8. Our Bayesian Inference (BI) method consistently outperforms the zero-shot baseline across all mod-els and metrics on the EmoScene dataset. Gemma-2-9b-it achieves the highest F1-Macro of 0.501 with lexical accuracy 83.48%, while Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct shows the largest gains (F1-Macro +5%, Hamming Loss, -6.275); improvements are most evident in smaller models such as Gemma-2-2b-it (lexical accuracy +7.858, Hamming –7.858), confirming BI boosts reliability and reduces per-emotion errors without affecting strong performers like Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. Vector accuracy remains modest (15–25%) de-spite high lexical scores because BI performs joint MAP inference over the full emotion vector E, en-forcing an entanglement prior via an MRF/Ising graph that models pairwise correlations (e.g., sad-ness co-occurs more with anger than joy). This corrects the independence assumption of zero-shot LLMs by re-weighting predictions to better match co-occurrence patterns and reduce Hamming Loss. 6.1 Predicitive Analysis 

Per-emotion precision & recall heatmaps can be found in Figure 4. Joy and Anticipation emerge as the strongest categories overall, where Joy achieves high precision and recall across all models consis-tently, while Anticipation shows moderate preci-sion and recall, reflecting their frequent occurrence in the dataset and the clear lexical cues that LLMs readily capture. In contrast, Disgust displays crit-ically low recall (near-zero in Llama and Mistral variants), and Surprise similarly suffers low recall in larger models, indicating models might be adopt-ing conservative strategies, predicting them only when evidence is overwhelming. In this multi-label setting, precision reflects the reliability of positive predictions (avoiding false cues like incorrectly detecting Anger), while recall ensures coverage of the full emotional spectrum. Smaller model like Gemma-2-2b-it show high re-call for Anticipation, Fear, Joy, Sadness, but low precision on labels like Anger and Surprise, indi-cating over-prediction on rare classes (e.g., Disgust, Surprise). Larger models such as Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct are more bal-anced but remain conservative on these emotions. This motivates our Bayesian post-processing: the MRF/Ising-based entanglement prior, which en-forces realistic co-occurrence patterns, improves Macro F1 and reduces Hamming Loss even for rare emotions without retraining. Below, we dis-cuss a general case where our posterior formulation corrected the label from the baseline. 

Scenario [Anticipation, Sadness] 

Sonia scrolled through her Instagram feed, her eyes narrowing as she paused on Frank’s latest post about his trip to Bali. Her stomach felt unsettled, and she noticed her muscles tensing as she read about his adventures. She couldn’t help but compare her own routine life to his glamorous lifestyle. Trying to shake off the feeling, she decided to focus on her own up-coming weekend plans and the things she has achieved recently. In the above scenario, the baseline incorrectly predicts Anger along with Anticipation and Sad-ness. In general, Sadness is a low-arousal negative emotion, whereas Anger is a high-arousal negative emotion. The description of her stomach being "unsettled" and muscles "tensing" injects a high level of physical arousal into the text. The base-line model struggled to map this high-arousal dis-comfort to Sadness, defaulting instead to Anger, which is the most common high-arousal negative emotion in text classification. 7 Conclusion and Future Work 

We introduced EmoScene , a context-rich bench-mark for evaluating multi-dimensional emotion understanding in language models. Grounded in Plutchik’s theory, EmoScene represents emotional states using an 8-dimensional emotion vector and contains 4,731 scenarios that require reasoning over contextual and relational cues. Our eval-uation of six instruction-tuned LLMs in a zero-shot setting shows that even strong models achieve modest performance, highlighting the difficulty of context-aware multi-label emotion prediction. To address the independence assumption commonly made in emotion classification, we proposed an entanglement-aware Bayesian inference framework that incorporates emotion co-occurrence statistics to perform joint posterior inference, improving pre-diction consistency and yielding notable gains for weaker models. These results suggest that mod-elling dependencies among emotions is important for robust emotion understanding. In future work, we plan to extend EmoScene with additional modal-ities and languages and investigate architectures and reasoning strategies that explicitly model emo-tional structure in context. Further, we would also like to explore the utility of preference tuning strate-gies in improving the emotional awareness of large language models. 

Limitations 

While EmoScene provides a context-rich bench-mark for multi-dimensional emotion understand-ing, several limitations remain. First, the scenarios are generated using a language model and may not fully capture the diversity and complexity of natu-rally occurring emotional narratives. Second, the dataset focuses solely on textual descriptions and does not incorporate multimodal signals such as facial expressions, speech, or physiological cues that often accompany emotional expression. Fi-nally, although our Bayesian inference framework models pairwise dependencies among emotions, it assumes relatively simple statistical relationships and may not capture higher-order emotional inter-actions present in real-world settings. Ethics Statement 

All snippets and examples used in this work were collected or generated following ethical practices and, where applicable, under permissive licenses. The dataset does not contain personally identifiable information or sensitive data. All human annota-tors involved in constructing the EmoScene dataset participated voluntarily and provided informed con-sent prior to annotation. The scenarios were generated using a language model and subsequently reviewed and annotated by human experts, with duplicates and inconsistent outputs removed during dataset curation. Additionally, generative AI tools were used only to improve the clarity and grammar of the manuscript during writing; they were not used for literature search, research design, or idea gen-eration. B Human Annotation 

All scenarios were annotated by three expert an-notators . The annotators are English teachers with prior annotation experience. Each annotator in-dependently labeled every scenario using the 8-dimensional emotion vector. Multiple emotions could be selected if the scenario expressed mixed emotional signals. The annotation was conducted using aspreadsheet-based interface. Annotators reported that labeling each scenario typically required approximately 2–5 minutes , depending on the complexity of the emotional cues present in the scenario. Annotation guidelines are clearly ex-plained and shared to all the annotators. Guidelines are also uploaded in the codebase that is shared. The final label for each scenario was determined using majority voting for each label among three annotators. To assess annotation consistency, we compute pairwise agreement between annotators using the Kappa. The agreement is Moderate ( κ

≈ 0.43) , indicating moderate consistency across annotators.
